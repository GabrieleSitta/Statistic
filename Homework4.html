Theory: the ideas behind the app
1. Mean and variance of one step. Bernoulli: μ = p, σ² = p(1 − p). Rademacher: μ = 0, σ² = 1. Both have finite variance, which is all the LLN and the CLT need.
2. Sums of independent steps. E[Sₙ] = nμ and, because the steps are independent, Var(Sₙ) = nσ² (the covariances are zero). Dividing a random variable by a constant c divides its mean by c and its variance by c².
3. Convergence in probability. Yₙ → c in probability if, for every ε > 0, P(|Yₙ − c| > ε) → 0. It does not say that every trajectory stays close to c after some point (that would be a deterministic limit): it says that the fraction of trajectories outside the band goes to zero.
4. Chebyshev and the weak LLN. P(|Y − E[Y]| > ε) ≤ Var(Y)/ε². With Y = Sₙ/n this gives P(|Sₙ/n − μ| > ε) ≤ σ²/(nε²) → 0, which proves Sₙ/n → μ in probability.
5. Central Limit Theorem. (Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) → N(0, 1) in distribution, i.e. P((Sₙ − nμ)/(σ√n) ≤ x) → Φ(x) for every x. For Rademacher μ = 0 and σ = 1, so the statistic is exactly Sₙ/√n.
6. Skewness. The standardized Bernoulli sum has skewness (1 − 2p)/√(np(1 − p)): with p far from ½ the histogram is lopsided for small n and becomes symmetric only slowly. Rademacher steps are symmetric, so their sums have skewness 0 for every n.
7. Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance. D = maxₓ |F̂(x) − Φ(x)| between the empirical distribution function of the m simulated values and the normal one. With m samples, values below about 1.36/√m are compatible with a normal law at the 5% level.
8. Several attackers. If m attackers act independently, the team total has mean mnμ and variance mnσ²; the team average per system and per attacker has variance σ²/(mn). A given system escapes all m Bernoulli attackers with probability (1 − p)ᵐ.

The penetration process

An attacker tries the n systems of a network one after another. Each attempt gives a random penetration score Xᵢ, and the attempts are independent and identically distributed. Adding them gives the attacker's score after i systems; the sequence S₁, S₂, …, Sₙ is the attacker's trajectory. With m attackers we observe m independent trajectories.

How n and m are used

  • n = number of systems = length of one trajectory. The theorems are about n → ∞, so n is increased: every run records the scores at many intermediate values of n (from 1 up to the chosen n), which is how the pages show the trend as the system grows.
  • m = number of attackers = number of independent trajectories. Each attacker gives one value of Sₙ, so the m attackers form the sample from which histograms, means and variances are estimated. A "suitable" m is one large enough for these estimates to be reliable: the standard error of an empirical mean is σ/√m times the scale of the quantity, so m = 800 already gives errors of a few percent.
  • The team. In tab 7 the m attackers are also considered together: their total score, the team rate per system and the fraction of the n systems penetrated by at least one of them.

Assumption: attackers and attempts are independent. Real attackers may share information or target the same weak systems; that is not modelled here.

What the LLN says and what it does not say

  • Bernoulli: the fraction of penetrated systems tends to p. With p = 0.15, a large network will have about 15% of its systems penetrated by each attacker, even if nobody can say which ones.
  • Rademacher: the average tends to 0. This does not mean that the total score goes to 0: Sₙ keeps wandering, but by about √n, which is small compared with n.
  • Not a deterministic limit. A single trajectory can leave the band again; the LLN only says that the probability of being outside goes to zero, which is what the second chart measures.

How many attackers are "suitable"?

The estimates of tabs 3–6 use the first k attackers. Larger k gives estimates closer to the theory: the error of the mean shrinks like 1/√k. This is what m changes: not the law of one attacker, but how precisely we can see it.

Interpretation of this run

    Conclusions

    1. Single steps stay random. Nothing in the simulation predicts the next attempt. Regularity appears only when many independent steps are added up.
    2. The average is predictable. Sₙ/n converges in probability to μ: p for Bernoulli, 0 for Rademacher. The probability of being farther than ε falls to zero, below the Chebyshev bound.
    3. The normalization decides what we see. Var(Sₙ) = nσ² grows, Var(Sₙ/n) = σ²/n vanishes, Var(Sₙ/√n) = σ² stays fixed.
    4. The fluctuations are normal. In the Rademacher case Sₙ/√n approaches N(0, 1), although each step is only ±1. For Bernoulli the same happens after centering, more slowly when p is far from ½.
    5. System size and attackers. A bigger network makes the absolute score larger and more variable, but the rate per system more predictable. More attackers raise the team total and its variance linearly in m, make the team rate even more concentrated, and quickly cover most of the network: with p = 0.15, 15 independent attackers already penetrate 90% of the systems on average.

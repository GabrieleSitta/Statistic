Ex. 1 — Four ways a sequence of random variables can converge

A random variable is a function X(ω) of the outcome ω, so "Xₙ gets close to X" can mean different things. In my examples X̄ₙ is the share of attacks among the first n connections seen by a sensor.

Almost sure (a.s.)

P( lim Xₙ = X ) = 1

For almost every outcome the sequence of numbers X₁(ω), X₂(ω), … converges. It is about whole trajectories: on the one stream of traffic that really happened, the running share of attacks ends up at the true share.

In probability

P( |Xₙ − X| > ε ) → 0

For every ε > 0, at each large n a deviation bigger than ε is unlikely. It looks at one n at a time: deviations may keep coming back later, on different outcomes.

In mean square (L²)

E[ (Xₙ − X)² ] → 0

The average squared error goes to 0. For an estimator this is the mean squared error (variance + bias²), so it is the mode used to judge estimators.

In distribution

FXₙ(x) → FX(x)

Only the distribution functions converge (where FX is continuous). The values need not get close: it is the shape of the histogram that converges.

Variances add, standard deviations do not

Var(X₁ + … + Xₙ) = Σᵢ Var(Xᵢ) + 2 Σi<j Cov(Xᵢ, Xⱼ)

If the Xᵢ are independent the covariances are 0 and the variance is additive, Var(Sₙ) = nσ². The size of the fluctuations is the standard deviation, so it grows only like the square root:

sd(Sₙ) = σ √n sd(X̄ₙ) = σ / √n

Independent errors partly cancel: the signal nμ grows like n, the noise like √n. If I divide the centred sum Sₙ − nμ by nᵅ, its spread is σ·n1/2 − α: with α = 1 it goes to 0 (LLN), with α = ½ it stays at σ and the CLT gives its normal shape, with α < ½ it explodes. So √n is the only scaling with a useful limit. The chart checks it on the 0/1 attack label.

√n in statistical inference

  • Standard error: SE(X̄ₙ) = σ/√n and SE(p̂) = √(p(1 − p)/n); the 95% interval X̄ₙ ± 1.96 s/√n comes from the CLT.
  • Sample size: the margin of error goes like 1/√n, so halving it needs four times the data. With a real number: the 2024 KnowBe4 benchmark says that 34.3% of untrained employees fail a phishing test. To estimate the rate of a company within ±m points at 95% I need n ≈ 1.96² · 0.343 · 0.657 / m²:

The role of independence

Without independence the covariances do not vanish. If the records are positively correlated, Var(X̄ₙ) is larger than σ²/n and the formula σ/√n gives intervals that are too narrow. In security data this is common: one scanner produces thousands of almost identical connections. A real example: the original KDD Cup 99 training set has 4,898,431 records but only 1,074,992 distinct ones (Tavallaee et al., 2009), about 78% copies. Copies add records but not information, so treating them as independent would overstate the precision. This is one reason why NSL-KDD, used on this page, removed the duplicates.

Ex. 2 — Conditional probability

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B) (P(B) > 0)

Knowing that B happened restricts the sample space to B. From the definition: the multiplication rule P(A ∩ B) = P(A | B) P(B), the law of total probability P(A) = Σⱼ P(A | Bⱼ) P(Bⱼ), and independence (Homework 7) as the case P(A | B) = P(A).

In probability theory

It is a modelling tool: I describe a process step by step (an attacker picks a target, then a technique, then succeeds or not) and compute joint probabilities with the chain rule.

In statistics

A model is a conditional distribution p(x | θ) of the data given the parameter. Frequentists keep θ fixed; Bayesians condition the other way, on the observed data. Confusing P(alarm | attack) with P(attack | alarm) is a classic error (tab 5).

Bayes' theorem: prior, likelihood, posterior

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E) P(E) = Σₖ P(E | Hₖ) P(Hₖ)
for a parameter: π(θ | x) ∝ p(x | θ) · π(θ)
Prior π(θ)what I believe before the data
×
Likelihood p(x | θ)how well each θ explains the data
→
Posterior π(θ | x)what I believe after
  • Prior: what I know about θ before the data (earlier data, benchmarks, expert opinion, or a vague choice).
  • Likelihood: the probability of the observed data as a function of θ. It is how the data enter, and it is not a distribution over θ.
  • Posterior: prior × likelihood, renormalized. From it I read an estimate (the mean), a 95% credible interval and the probability of any hypothesis.

With two hypotheses: posterior odds = likelihood ratio × prior odds.

Why updating is central

If new data arrive, the posterior obtained so far becomes the prior for the next step: π(θ | x₁, x₂) ∝ p(x₂ | θ) · π(θ | x₁). The final result does not depend on the order of the data or on how they are split. So Bayesian inference is one rule applied again and again, which fits security well: the belief about a host or a campaign is revised every time a new alert or log arrives. Example 1: what is an IDS alarm worth? (Axelsson, 2000)

In his paper on the base-rate fallacy Axelsson considers about 1,000,000 audit records per day, 2 intrusions per day and about 10 records per intrusion. So the prior probability that a record belongs to an intrusion is P(I) = 20/10⁶ = 2·10⁻⁵. The detector gives the likelihoods: the detection rate P(A | I) and the false-alarm rate P(A | ¬I) (change them in the sidebar).

P(I | A) = P(A | I) P(I) / [ P(A | I) P(I) + P(A | ¬I) P(¬I) ]

Updating with more detectors

If a second detector with the same error rates, whose errors are independent of the first, also raises an alarm, the posterior of the first step becomes the prior of the second.

The independence assumption matters: two detectors with the same signature make the same mistakes, so the second alarm adds almost nothing.

Example 2: the success rate of a phishing e-mail (Jagatic et al., 2007)

In a real experiment at Indiana University phishing e-mails were sent to students. In the control group (unknown sender) 15 of 94 students (16%) entered their credentials; in the "social" group (the e-mail seemed to come from a friend) 349 of 487 (72%). I estimate the unknown rate θ with a Beta prior and a binomial likelihood:

likelihood ∝ θᵏ (1 − θ)ⁿ⁻ᵏ prior Beta(a, b) ∝ θᵃ⁻¹ (1 − θ)ᵇ⁻¹ ⇒ posterior Beta(a + k, b + n − k)

The update just adds the successes to a and the failures to b, so a + b works like a number of "earlier e-mails". Priors (sidebar): flat; one from the 2024 KnowBe4 benchmark (34.3% of untrained employees fail a test) worth 20 e-mails; and an over-confident one from the same report (4.6% after one year of training) worth 100 e-mails, which describes a different population.

    Exercise 2: advantages, limitations and influence of the prior

    • Advantages. Direct answers (a probability for θ or for a hypothesis), prior knowledge used explicitly, reasonable results with few data, and updating step by step as new data arrive.
    • The prior can decide the result when the data are few, and a prior from the wrong population (trained employees in 2024 against students in 2005) can mislead. A table with several priors, like the one in tab 6, should always be shown.
    • The base rate must be right. In the IDS example the posterior changes completely with the prior.
    • The likelihood is an assumption too. The binomial assumes independent clicks, but friends warn each other; then the posterior is too narrow.
    • Computation. Here the prior is conjugate and the posterior has a formula; in general it needs numerical methods.
    • Link with exercise 1. The posterior standard deviation shrinks like √(θ(1 − θ)/n): the same 1/√n as the standard error, so with many data every reasonable prior gives the same answer.

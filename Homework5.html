Theory: the ideas behind the app
1. Estimator. A rule that turns the data into a guess of an unknown parameter. x̄ guesses μ, s² guesses σ². The estimate is random, because it depends on the sample.
2. Unbiased. An estimator is unbiased if its expected value is the parameter: E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ². Averaging the estimates of many independent samples (here: many hackers) should therefore give back μ and σ².
3. Why n − 1. The deviations xᵢ − x̄ are measured from the sample mean, which is as close to the data as possible, so SS underestimates Σ(xᵢ − μ)². On average E[SS] = (n − 1)σ²: one degree of freedom is spent on x̄. Dividing by n − 1 (Bessel's correction) removes the bias; dividing by n gives E[SS/n] = (n − 1)σ²/n.
4. Variability of x̄. Var(x̄) = σ²/n, so its standard deviation (standard error) is σ/√n. In practice σ is unknown and s/√n is used.
5. Variability of s². Var(s²) = (μ₄ − (n − 3)σ⁴/(n − 1)) / n, where μ₄ = E[(X − μ)⁴]. It also falls like 1/n, but it depends on the fourth moment: distributions with heavy tails or extreme values (like all-or-nothing) give much noisier variance estimates.
6. Consistency. An unbiased estimator whose variance goes to 0 as n grows converges in probability to the parameter (Chebyshev). Both x̄ and s² are consistent.
7. Confidence interval. x̄ ± t·s/√n with t the 97.5% quantile of Student's t with n − 1 degrees of freedom contains μ in about 95% of the samples. Using 1.96 instead of t is correct only for large n.

Ex. 1 — Welford's recurrence formulas

The goal is to update the mean, the sum of squares and the variance one value at a time, without storing the data and without the numerically dangerous formula Σx² − (Σx)²/k. The idea is the same as Knuth's formula for the mean: write the quantity after k values in terms of the same quantity after k − 1 values plus a correction that depends only on the new value.

Notation, for the first k values x₁, …, xₖ

x̄ₖ = (1/k) Σ xᵢ

running mean

SSₖ = Σ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ)²

sum of squares around the mean

s²ₖ = SSₖ / (k − 1)

sample variance, k ≥ 2

The new value enters every formula only through its innovation δₖ = xₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁, the distance from the mean before the update.

1

Knuth's recurrence for the mean

Multiply the mean by k and separate the last value:

k·x̄ₖ = x₁ + … + xₖ₋₁ + xₖ = (k − 1)·x̄ₖ₋₁ + xₖ
x̄ₖ = x̄ₖ₋₁ + (xₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁)/k = x̄ₖ₋₁ + δₖ / k

Two facts follow and are used below:

(a) x̄ₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁ = δₖ / kthe mean moves by 1/k of the innovation
(b) xₖ − x̄ₖ = δₖ − δₖ/k = (k − 1)·δₖ / kdistance of the new value from the new mean
2

A shift rule for sums of squares

For any constant c, the squares around c equal the squares around the mean plus a correction (König–Huygens). Applied to the first k − 1 values:

Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ (xᵢ − c)² = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ ((xᵢ − x̄ₖ₋₁) + (x̄ₖ₋₁ − c))²
= SSₖ₋₁ + 2(x̄ₖ₋₁ − c)·Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ₋₁) + (k − 1)(x̄ₖ₋₁ − c)²
= SSₖ₋₁ + (k − 1)(x̄ₖ₋₁ − c)²the middle sum is 0: deviations from a mean add up to zero
3

Welford's recurrence for SS

Split SSₖ into the first k − 1 terms and the last one, and use the shift rule with c = x̄ₖ together with (a) and (b):

SSₖ = Σᵢ₌₁ᵏ⁻¹ (xᵢ − x̄ₖ)² + (xₖ − x̄ₖ)²
= SSₖ₋₁ + (k − 1)(δₖ/k)² + ((k − 1)δₖ/k)²
= SSₖ₋₁ + (k − 1)δₖ²/k² · (1 + (k − 1))
= SSₖ₋₁ + (k − 1)/k · δₖ²

By (b), (k − 1)δₖ/k is exactly xₖ − x̄ₖ, which gives the form used in code:

SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ + δₖ · (xₖ − x̄ₖ) = SSₖ₋₁ + (xₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁)(xₖ − x̄ₖ), SS₁ = 0

The correction is a product of the distances of the new value from the old and from the new mean: it is never negative, so SS can only grow, as it should.

4

Recurrence for the variance

Substitute SSₖ = (k − 1)s²ₖ and SSₖ₋₁ = (k − 2)s²ₖ₋₁ and divide by k − 1:

(k − 1)s²ₖ = (k − 2)s²ₖ₋₁ + (k − 1)δₖ²/k
s²ₖ = ((k − 2)/(k − 1))·s²ₖ₋₁ + δₖ²/k, k ≥ 2 (so s²₂ = δ₂²/2)

The same steps give the version with divisor k (the "population" variance σ̂²ₖ = SSₖ/k), used in tab 5 to show the bias:

σ̂²ₖ = ((k − 1)/k)·σ̂²ₖ₋₁ + (k − 1)δₖ²/k²

The core logic: three lines

// for each new score x, with k = number of scores seen so far (1, 2, 3, …)
const d = x - mean;        // δₖ = xₖ − x̄ₖ₋₁
mean += d / k;             // Knuth:   x̄ₖ = x̄ₖ₋₁ + δₖ/k
ss   += d * (x - mean);    // Welford: SSₖ = SSₖ₋₁ + δₖ(xₖ − x̄ₖ)

// then, whenever needed:  s² = ss / (k − 1)   (k ≥ 2)
// equivalent one-line update of the variance itself:
// v = k < 2 ? 0 : v * (k - 2) / (k - 1) + d * d / k;

Memory O(1) and one pass over the data: the scores of a hacker can be processed as they arrive. The same three lines are used everywhere in this page, both for the n scores of each hacker and, a second time, across the m hackers.

Why not Σx² − (Σx)²/n?

The "textbook" one-pass formula s² = (Σx² − (Σx)²/n)/(n − 1) is algebraically the same, but it subtracts two huge, almost equal numbers. A double has about 16 significant digits: when the data sit far from 0, the difference is lost in rounding. Below, the same example is shifted by a large constant. Shifting does not change the variance, so every method should give the same value.

Ex. 2 — The score of one penetration attempt

The previous homework gave each attempt a 0/1 (or ±1) outcome. Here every attempt gets a score between 0 and 10, like a severity score: 0 = nothing gained, 10 = full control of the system. The score is drawn from a fixed distribution with mean μ and variance σ². Four distributions are available in the sidebar, chosen to behave differently.

Why these four distributions

  • Mostly low scores (default): a discrete pmf on {0, …, 10} that decreases from 22% at 0 to 4% at 10. Most attempts gain little, a few are critical, which is realistic for well-defended systems. It is right-skewed.
  • Triangular(0, 10) with mode 7: a continuous score, most often around 7, never outside [0, 10]. It is left-skewed and has no extreme values.
  • All-or-nothing: 0 with probability 0.7 and 10 with probability 0.3. The largest possible variance for its mean and a large fourth moment: the hardest case for estimating σ².
  • Binomial(10, 0.6): the number of successes in 10 independent sub-steps, each with probability 0.6. Almost symmetric and close to normal.

The theoretical μ, σ² and μ₄ are computed exactly from the distribution (for the triangular one by numerical integration) and shown in the sidebar.

Each hacker attacks n systems and gets n scores. Welford gives the hacker's sampling mean x̄ⱼ and sampling variance s²ⱼ; then the same three lines, applied to the m values x̄₁ … x̄ₘ and s²₁ … s²ₘ, give their average and their spread.

What the comparisons show

    Conclusions

    1. The averages match μ and σ²: the estimators are unbiased

    For every n, even 2 or 5, the average of the m sampling means is close to μ and the average of the m sampling variances is close to σ². What is left is Monte Carlo noise: it is smaller than a couple of standard errors and shrinks if m is increased. This is the meaning of E[x̄] = μ and E[s²] = σ². It holds only with the divisor n − 1; SS/n is systematically too small by the factor (n − 1)/n.

    2. Why this matters for inferential statistics

    In practice μ and σ² are unknown and we see one sample, not m. Unbiasedness guarantees that the procedure has no systematic error: if it were repeated many times, it would be right on average. Knowing also the spread, Var(x̄) = σ²/n, we can say how far one estimate is likely to be from the truth: the standard error s/√n, confidence intervals x̄ ± t·s/√n and tests are built exactly on these two facts. The coverage column shows that such an interval does contain μ about 95% of the time once n is not tiny (for very small n, or for all-or-nothing scores, the coverage is lower, because s itself is then a poor estimate of σ). Welford makes all of this computable online, one score at a time, without storing the data and without rounding problems.

    3. More systems per hacker: less variability

    Increasing n does not move the averages, which stay at μ and σ², but makes the individual estimates much more similar to each other. The standard deviation of x̄ falls like σ/√n: 100 times more systems give a 10 times tighter estimate. The standard deviation of s² also falls like 1/√n, but with a larger constant that depends on the fourth moment μ₄, so the variance is always harder to estimate than the mean, especially for distributions with extreme values. For small n the distribution of s² is also skewed; for large n both x̄ and s² become approximately normal and concentrate on the true values (consistency).
